The curriculum architecture

Nine connected inquiry cycles provide a possible sequence across an academic year. Each can expand, recur, or pause in response to children’s questions and educator observations. This is a scope-and-sequence overview, not nine fully written units or a fixed pacing calendar.

Six recurring learning strands

  • Communication & emergent literacy: Explain an idea with speech, gesture, drawing, home language, or assistive communication.
  • Mathematical thinking: Compare quantities, shapes, positions, and patterns in meaningful play.
  • Inquiry & problem-solving: Notice, predict, test, compare, and change an approach.
  • Social participation: Join shared work, negotiate roles, and seek or offer support.
  • Physical development: Choose and use accessible materials and tools with growing coordination.
  • Representation & creative expression: Use materials, movement, sound, or marks to communicate an idea.

Planning rhythm: Notice children’s ideas → choose a useful invitation → observe and document → interpret with colleagues and children → plan the next invitation. Revisit earlier ideas rather than treating a cycle as a finished topic.

DETAILED SAMPLE · 10 INVITATIONS

Build, Test, Rebuild

Driving question: What makes a structure stand?

Intent: Children explore how a base, material, or arrangement affects a structure; represent an idea; and take part in shared problem-solving. Look for changes in their strategies and explanations across repeated opportunities.

Materials: Large blocks and age-appropriate construction pieces, broad base shapes, large toy figures or vehicles, paper, thick mark-making tools, photos of children’s own constructions, and optional camera access managed by the educator. Select pieces appropriate to the youngest participating child and local safety practices.

Daily rhythm: A short optional gathering introduces the invitation; a substantial period of choice-based exploration follows; a brief revisit makes children’s thinking visible. These invitations can overlap or take more than one day. Avoid turning the sequence into a required identical product.

DayInvitationEducator promptEvidence to notice
1Notice and build
Offer a small selection of large blocks. Invite children to build something that stands.		What do you notice about the bottom?Record one construction and the child’s explanation; do not correct it into an adult model.
2Compare bases
Offer two broad base shapes and familiar blocks. Compare what children choose.		What changed when you tried that base?Capture a comparison through pointing, speech, photo, or drawing.
3Test a prediction
Invite a choice: wide or narrow base, same building pieces. Model a gentle, consistent test.		Which do you think will stay standing? How could we find out?Record prediction separately from observed result.
4Change one thing
Revisit a documented structure. Invite children to change one feature and try again.		What would you like to change first?Describe the specific change and the child’s response.
5Tell the story
Offer photos and drawing materials to revisit the week.		What happened before? What did you try next?Collect a dictated, drawn, signed, or spoken sequence.
6Build together
Introduce a shared design space and invite children to choose roles.		How could both ideas fit?Record a negotiation or a support offered, without assigning a personality label.
7Solve a new problem
Offer a toy vehicle or large figure and invite a bridge or shelter that fits it.		How will we know there is enough room?Note a comparison, measurement strategy, or revision.
8Plan and represent
Offer a choice of sketching, arranging sample pieces, or describing a plan before building.		How could you show someone your idea?Compare the representation with the construction, without requiring an exact match.
9Transfer and adapt
Offer one different construction material while keeping the purpose familiar.		What works differently with this material?Record whether a prior idea is reused, adapted, or set aside.
10Curate and share
Children select a photo, model, or drawing to share with a peer or family.		What would you like someone to know about your work?Create a short learning story with the child’s words and an educator’s next-step note.

Access and responsive support

  • Entry: Offer fewer, larger pieces; a stable work surface; side-by-side participation; and a model of exploring rather than a model to copy.
  • Extension: Invite a new constraint, a comparison of two arrangements, a representation, or a second trial. Follow readiness and interest rather than age alone.
  • Language: Pair gestures and photographs with words such as base, above, below, balance, and change. Welcome home-language explanations and adult transcription.
  • Participation: Provide floor or table access, materials that can be positioned with support, quieter work areas, and alternatives to speaking in a group.

Interpretation before the next plan

Compare observations across at least two invitations. Ask: What strategy did the child use? What support changed participation? What could we offer next? A single collapsed tower or short conversation is not a developmental judgment.

A fictional example

Observation: During block play, Child A placed a broad block under a narrow tower after two earlier falls and said, “This one is the feet.”

Possible interpretation: The child may be connecting a broader base with stability. This needs another opportunity to observe, rather than a mastery label.

Next invitation: Offer the same pieces and two different bases; invite a choice and a prediction using speech, gesture, or drawing.

Family connection · English / Español

What can you build together?

We are exploring how structures stand, fall, and change. Children are trying ideas, noticing what happens, and deciding what to try next.

If you wish, build together using safe materials already available at home. Ask, “What do you notice?” or “What would you like to change?” A conversation, gesture, or story is enough; there is no worksheet to complete or material to purchase.

You are welcome to share a few words about your child’s idea in your home language. Please follow the setting’s guidance if sharing photographs.

¿Qué pueden construir juntos?

Estamos explorando cómo las estructuras se sostienen, se caen y cambian. Los niños prueban ideas, observan lo que sucede y deciden qué intentar después.

Si lo desean, pueden construir juntos con materiales seguros que ya tengan en casa. Pregunten: «¿Qué observas?» o «¿Qué te gustaría cambiar?». Basta con una conversación, un gesto o un relato; no hay una ficha para completar ni materiales para comprar.

Pueden compartir unas palabras sobre la idea de su hijo o hija en el idioma que hablan en casa. Si comparten fotografías, sigan las indicaciones del centro.
